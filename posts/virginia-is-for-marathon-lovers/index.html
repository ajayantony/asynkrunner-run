Virginia Is for (Marathon) Lovers

14 Nov, 2026

Another Training Block in the Books

Twenty weeks, give or take, plus a few 20-mile weeks I’d already strung together before the official plan even started. By race day, Garmin had the full picture: 715.74 miles and 114 hours 27 minutes on my feet between June 4th and November 14th. Peak week landed at 51 miles, with a 21-mile long run as the biggest single effort before taper. Over the block, my lactate threshold pace dropped from 8:37/mile to 7:59/mile, and VO2 max ticked up from 45 to 48. Whatever that’s worth on race day (and as you’ll see, it’s not everything), the fitness was clearly there.

Virginia Is for Lovers (of Long Drives)

With the training banked, it was time to head south, to the self-proclaimed state for lovers, Virginia. It’s about an 8-hour drive from home, so we broke it up. We left after work and stopped for the night in Harrisburg, PA, at the Hampton Inn & Suites Harrisburg/North right off I-81, about as conveniently located off a highway as a hotel can be.

The next morning we got an early start toward Shenandoah National Park, about two hours out. We drove Skyline Drive just past peak fall color, but it was still a gorgeous ride. No family trip through a national park is complete without the obligatory hike, so we did the one-hour loop up Stony Man, an easy trail that even my 8-year-old handled without complaint. The view from the top, looking down over the valley, was worth every step. Stony Man happens to be the second-highest peak in Shenandoah National Park (after Hawksbill), a nice bit of trivia to carry back down the mountain.

From there, it was on to the Quirk Hotel in Richmond. After my last race, the Vermont City Marathon, I made myself a rule: stay as close to the start line as possible, and “close” means walkable. Quirk delivered: a 10-minute walk to the start.

Quirk Hotel and Dinner at Stella’s

Quirk lives up to its name and its spot in Richmond’s arts district, a few blocks from a woven steel face mounted on the side of a building: “The Thin Blue Line,” a sculpture by Michael Stutz honoring the Richmond Police Department.

We had dinner reservations at Stella’s, an upscale Greek spot nearby. If you go, get the whole roasted fish. I had the branzino, and it did not disappoint.

Brunch, Bibs, and a Carb-Loading Mistake

The next morning, while the family slowly came to life, I snuck out for a shakeout run, a quick 2 miles on the loop near Monroe Park. Felt good. I was starting to believe I was ready for what was coming.

Back at the hotel, everyone was up and hungry, so we headed to Perly’s, a Jewish deli with great reviews that passed my usual test of scrolling through the food photos people post on Google first. I’ve got a knack for telling whether a dish is actually good just from the photo, and more often than not it points me straight to what to order. Per the reviews, it usually has a wait, so the girls went ahead while I showered. By the time I caught up, we were already being seated. Everything we ordered was excellent. The corned beef hash was the best I’ve ever had, and the potato latkes were finger-licking good. In hindsight, the problem wasn’t that brunch replaced two separate meals, it’s that none of it was an actual carb-load. Hash and latkes are delicious, but they’re mostly protein and fat, and the day before a marathon calls for a deliberate pile of rice, pasta, or bread.

After brunch, we swung by Richmond Raceway for bib pickup, smooth, easy, and full of the freebies kids love. Then it was on to Carytown, apparently a must-do in Richmond: a cute strip with a Yeti scavenger hunt the kids attempted (and partially won, a few Yetis were found). We also swung by Crispy Cones, the rotisserie-grilled ice cream cone shop that landed Barbara Corcoran as an investor on Shark Tank, which had only opened its first Virginia location right there in Carytown a few months before we showed up. The kids have picked up a habit of watching Shark Tank whenever we travel, since hotel rooms always seem to have cable and home doesn’t, and they were thrilled to stumble onto an actual Shark Tank product in the wild.

We had dinner at Tulsi, an Indian restaurant with genuinely fantastic interior design. Richmond does art well, and this place was no exception, which tracks with basically every wall we passed in this city.

The food itself didn’t blow us away, though the salmon tandoori stood out. White rice and salmon is a perfectly fine dinner, just not the carb-load I needed. We also spent most of the day on our feet instead of off them, mistake number two, and by the time we got back to Quirk around 7 PM to lay out the race gear, the nerves were setting in for real.

I got about 6 hours of sleep, woke up at 5 AM, and ate a banana, a Honey Stinger waffle, and some gummies. Three mistakes, or so I thought at the time: not enough actual carbohydrate the day before, too much time on my feet, and not enough sleep. Stack those three together and you’ve got a bonk waiting to happen. Turns out that wasn’t the whole story, but more on that later.

Race Day

The race started at 7 AM. I jogged from the hotel at 6:30, which doubled as my warmup and, bonus, meant I skipped the porta-potty line entirely. Someone, somewhere, needs to solve the porta-potty math for road races. I’ve yet to run one where that line isn’t painfully long.

Starting in wave 3, I was off about two minutes after the gun. Temperatures were right around 30°F at the start, and for once I got the clothing call right: no base layer, shorts, a short-sleeve shirt, arm sleeves, thin gloves, no hat. Arm sleeves are magic: roll them down the second you warm up and you’re set.

The size of the field kept things conservative early, which was fine by me. I settled into a steady sub-4-hour pace, around 9:10/mile, and held it through mile 15. I felt good through mile 18, and then the wheels started coming off a little more with every passing mile.

My watch had me averaging 10:01/mile for the race (official chip pace came in a touch slower at 10:08, the usual GPS-vs-timing-mat gap), with one mile somewhere early clocking in at 7:13 before reality set back in. Average heart rate sat at 159 bpm, maxing out at 181 bpm.

Do the math on that split and it’s not subtle. If I was really on 9:10 pace through mile 15, that’s about 2:17:24 elapsed. The remaining 11.2 miles ate up the rest of the race, which works out to roughly 11:25/mile for the back half. That’s not fading. That’s the wall. Blend those two paces across the full 26.2 and you land almost exactly on the official 10:08/mile average, so the data and the memory agree on what happened.

I stuck to my in-race fueling plan the whole way, a gel every 30 minutes alternating Huma Electrolyte+ and Huma Ultra, roughly 65 grams of carbs an hour. My feet simply refused to cooperate in the back half anyway, and I ended up taking more walk breaks than I’d have liked.

I’d hoped to finish under 4 hours. I crossed the line at 4:25:20 instead.

Winding Down

I found my family at the finish, but the weather had turned colder and nobody wanted to linger. The post-race party was low-key, just pizza and beer, and for once, I had zero interest in the beer. We took the shuttle back toward the start and walked to the hotel to clean up.

Dinner that night was at Sweet P’s, American food with a Southern twist. The Brussels sprout appetizer and the short rib sandwich were both outstanding. Afterward, we walked over to the famous “Greetings from Richmond” mural for a few photos before heading back to the hotel to collapse in front of the TV.

We were all wiped out and asleep early, with an early start planned for the morning. We left Richmond around 8 AM the next day and made it home by about 5:30 PM, tired, well-fed, and with Virginia checked off the list.

What Actually Caused the Bonk

If you’re just here for the trip, thanks for reading, and feel free to stop here. If you want my best theory for what actually went wrong, keep going. This isn’t a coach’s verdict, just my own read on my training log after the fact, and I could be wrong about parts of it. My first instinct was the day-before nutrition and the six hours of sleep, but digging in further, I think those were the last straw, not the root cause. The gaps I think actually mattered were baked into the training cycle itself, months before Richmond.

I did plenty of marathon-pace work this cycle, just never inside a long run. It lived in its own separate pace sessions on fresh legs, days apart from anything long. My long runs also had a habit I never thought twice about: stopping every 5 miles or so for a bathroom break, water, or a gel. Stopping resets your legs and your breathing in a way a race never lets you. Between the two, I never once rehearsed holding marathon pace on genuinely tired legs. Mile 18 on race day was the first time I’d ever tried.

The fueling cadence had the same problem. My long runs ran at a slower ~9:45/mile, so I fueled on an hourly schedule, which is appropriate for that pace. The actual race called for a gel every 30 minutes to hit roughly 65g of carbs an hour, something I only rehearsed at full rate a single time all cycle. Gut absorption at a given intake rate improves with repetition, and mine had barely been asked to do it.

Then there’s the shoe. My last marathon was in the ASICS Magic Speed, a plastic-plated racing shoe, not quite a true super shoe but still built for speed. This one I ran in the ASICS Superblast 2, a plateless max-cushion trainer, almost an ounce heavier per shoe with none of the propulsive assist, and I’d never raced or long-run in it before. Plated shoes, even a plastic-plated one, are generally measured at a small improvement in running economy over plateless trainers. Small per mile, but it adds up over 26.2, and it costs the most exactly where I fell apart: the fatigued second half, when every bit of lost spring has to get made up with extra muscle work.

None of this was a fitness problem. This was my highest mileage week ever at 51 miles, the lactate threshold and VO2 max numbers back up that the fitness was real, and almost every one of those miles happened in the evening after work, same as always. Race morning started at 7 AM, just two hours after waking, on an overnight fast, nothing like the hundred or so evening runs behind it. Pace, fatigue, fueling cadence, shoes, even the time of day: every one of those variables had been trained separately, and race day was the first time all of them showed up together, at once, for 26.2 miles.

That’s my theory, anyway, built from staring at my own Garmin data a little too long after the fact. If you’ve bonked in a marathon before and have a different read on what actually causes it, or think I’m missing something obvious here, I’d genuinely like to hear it. Drop a comment below.

Lessons for Next Time

Writing this down mostly for future-me, but hopefully useful for anyone else about to make the same mistakes.

Carb-load by grams, not by meal count. A big brunch isn’t a carb-load if the big brunch is corned beef hash and latkes. The day before a marathon needs a deliberate pile of rice, pasta, or bread, not just a full stomach.

Train race day as one combined thing, not five separate skills. Pace, fueling cadence, fatigue, and even time of day all need to show up in the same long run at some point in the buildup, not as isolated sessions on fresh legs. That means no full stops for breaks, fueling on the exact race-day schedule more than once, and getting a few key long runs onto race morning’s clock instead of always running in the evening.

If I’m changing shoes from the one that worked last time, that change gets its own long run and ideally a tune-up race first. Not its debut on marathon day.

Sleep gets the same priority as the carb-load, not an afterthought. Six hours the night before a marathon isn’t enough, race nerves or not.

And the day before still counts as taper. Sightseeing, bib pickup, a Yeti scavenger hunt, it all adds up, and being off my feet applies to the full day, not just race morning.

None of this cost me finishing. It cost me the sub-4. Worth fixing before the next one.
